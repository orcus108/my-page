title: What If the Future Goes Right?

date: 2026-07-31
read_time: 8 min read
summary: The upside is underrated
---

Most conversations about the future are weirdly pessimistic.

AI takes your job. Climate makes half the planet uninhabitable. Governments surveil everyone. A few corporations own everything. People become dependent, lonely husks.

Some of these fears are legitimate. A few might even turn out worse than we expect. But we spend so much time stress-testing the downside that we barely think about the upside, and I think the upside is much bigger than most people realize.

Here's the thing: for most of human history, progress has been constrained by a handful of things being genuinely scarce. Intelligence. Energy. Skilled labour. Good teachers. Good doctors. Scientific experiments that take years to run. Building anything physical is expensive. Biology is complicated and barely understood.

We treat these limits as permanent. They might not be.

---

**Intelligence has always required a human being.**

If you wanted to solve a hard problem, someone had to understand it. Start a company, write software, design a machine, analyse a law, you needed people with the right knowledge and enough time. That placed a natural ceiling on what a small group could accomplish.

AI starts to change this. Not because it becomes some omniscient oracle. It'll probably remain unreliable and narrow in specific ways for a long time. But imperfect intelligence is still enormously useful when it's cheap, fast, and always available.

A student gets a tutor who knows exactly where they're confused. A scientist tests a thousand hypotheses instead of ten. A small team does work that used to require hundreds of people. Someone with no formal training turns an idea into a product.

The interesting part isn't that AI is powerful. It's that human progress is often not bottlenecked by a lack of ideas. It's bottlenecked by the time, money, and attention needed to act on them. If intelligence becomes more abundant, a lot of things that are currently impossible become merely expensive, and then eventually ordinary.

---

**Software changed the world by manipulating information. But most of the real world isn't made of information.**

Houses need to be built. Crops need to grow. Old people need help. Dangerous places need to be inspected. Goods need to move.

Machines have always struggled with this because the physical world is messy. A factory robot can repeat one movement perfectly. A robot in a normal home has to deal with objects in the wrong place, bad lighting, kids walking around, a thousand situations nobody planned for.

But if robots become more general-purpose, if you can tell a machine what needs to happen rather than programming every step, physical labour starts to gain some of the same properties as software. Easier to copy. Easier to improve. Easier to scale.

Construction gets faster and cheaper. Dangerous jobs get done by machines. Farms use less water and fewer chemicals. Manufacturing moves closer to where products are actually needed. None of this is inevitable, but the direction is clear.

---

**Medicine today is impressive, but it's mostly reactive.**

Something goes wrong in the body. We detect it. We try to slow down the damage.

But biology is made of instructions. Genes tell cells what to do. Cells communicate with each other. The immune system follows rules, even if those rules are extraordinarily complicated. We're slowly learning how to read and rewrite those instructions: gene editing to fix diseases at the source rather than manage symptoms forever, training immune cells to fight cancer, growing replacement tissue, designing microbes that produce food or medicine or materials.

This is hard. Biology is far more complicated than software. A small mistake in a program crashes an app; a small mistake in biology can harm a person. But the direction still matters. Some diseases we currently treat as permanent facts of life may turn out to be technical problems we hadn't yet figured out how to solve.

---

**Everything also depends on energy.**

Clean water requires energy. Food production requires energy. Cooling cities, running computation, making things, recycling things, all energy. When energy is expensive, lots of solutions that theoretically work remain practically impossible.

You can remove salt from seawater, but it takes energy. You can pull carbon from the atmosphere, but it takes energy. You can run enormous computers to accelerate scientific research, but they take energy.

Solar is getting cheaper fast. Batteries are improving. Nuclear might have a real moment. Geothermal is being explored in new ways. We probably don't need a single magic breakthrough. Several technologies improving simultaneously might be enough. And if energy becomes genuinely cheap and abundant, a whole category of problems that currently make no economic sense suddenly become solvable. Hot places can afford more cooling. Regions without fresh water can use desalination. Places that skipped industrialization don't have to repeat every dirty stage of it.

Energy abundance converts a lot of scarcity problems into engineering problems.

---

**The most underrated piece of this might be education.**

The basic structure of school has barely changed. One teacher explains the same thing to a large group. Everyone moves at the same pace. Some kids are bored; some are lost; most learn enough to pass and forget most of it.

This isn't because it's the ideal system. It's because one teacher physically cannot personally teach thirty students at once.

A good personal tutor works completely differently. They notice when you're confused. They explain the same idea from a different angle. They know when to give you a hint and when to let you sit with the difficulty. They remember what you covered last week. They adjust their pace based on how quickly you're getting it.

That kind of education has always existed, for the small number of people wealthy enough to access it. If AI makes it widely available, the biggest effect probably isn't that students finish faster. It's that millions of people discover they're capable of things they never thought they could do. Someone who struggled with maths their whole life might find out the problem wasn't a lack of ability. It was just a bad fit with how they were being taught.

---

**The really interesting thing is how these changes interact.**

AI helps scientists design better medicines. Robots run experiments around the clock. Cheap energy powers more computation and manufacturing. Better education puts more people in a position to contribute. Each improvement accelerates the others.

The time between an idea and a working solution shrinks.

Right now, discovering a new drug takes years. Large-scale projects require enormous teams. Learning a difficult skill takes months before someone becomes useful. What happens when those loops get much faster? Humanity gets dramatically better at learning. Progress happens when you try something, observe what happened, learn from it, and try again. Faster experiments mean faster learning, better tools mean more people can participate, and the whole thing compounds.

Maybe we're approaching something unlike any period of progress we've seen before.

---

**None of this is automatic, though.**

Technology expands the space of possible futures. It does not choose among them.

AI could make education widely available, or it could erode people's ability to think. Personalised medicine could become universal, or it could remain a luxury. Cheap energy could create abundance, or it could be captured by a small group. Robots could eliminate dangerous work, or the wealth they generate could flow almost entirely upward.

We still have to make choices. We need institutions that can adapt instead of just resisting. We need companies that build for people rather than extracting attention from them. We need to think seriously about who owns these systems, who benefits from them, and who gets left behind.

There will be failures. There will be misuse. Some things that look revolutionary today will go nowhere. And I want to be honest: uncertainty works in both directions. The future could be worse than we expect.

But it could also be much better.

---

Pessimism often sounds more intelligent because every positive claim has an obvious attack. It's easy to explain why something will fail. Most things do fail. Every technology has limits. Every institution has bad incentives. Every optimistic prediction can be made to sound naïve.

But civilisation was built by people who looked at problems they couldn't yet solve and decided they weren't permanent.

Flight was impossible until it wasn't. Diseases that killed millions are now prevented with a small injection. A device in your pocket accesses more information than any library in history. Billions of people can talk to each other instantly.

None of that means progress is guaranteed. It means the limits of the present are not the limits of the future.

If we use these tools well, and that's a real if, we could get fewer diseases that can't be cured, less dangerous work, less of humanity's effort spent just surviving. More people able to turn an idea into a real thing. Less determined by the accident of where you were born.

That future isn't inevitable. But it's possible. And maybe we should spend a little more time thinking about what happens if it goes right.
